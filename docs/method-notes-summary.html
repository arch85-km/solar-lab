Method Notes in Brief

The short version: what the tool computes, and — the part that matters most when you cite it — what it does not model at all. It traces sun and sky against your geometry; it does not bounce light, and that single absence shapes every limitation below. The full method notes give the measurements, the source values and the references behind every statement here.

Four things to know before you cite a number

  1. What the eye sees and what the number reports are two different machineries. The shadows in the viewport are a GPU shadow map; the analysis is a ray test against a bounding-volume hierarchy, run in a worker. They can disagree at the edges, and when they do the number is the one to trust.
  2. With no climate file loaded, every figure is a synthetic clear day. The fallback is a fair-weather upper bound with no cloud in it at all. A kWh/m² total produced that way is not a climate result and must never be quoted as one.
  3. The OpenStreetMap context shades the analysis but is never measured. Sensors come from your model alone, so the neighbours cast onto it and receive nothing. The switch that lets them shade is on by default — so a run with context loaded is already a shaded run.
  4. Only the near ring of context casts a viewport shadow. Buildings within 160 m are in the shadow map; the rest are not. The ray tracer sees all of them regardless, so the numbers account for buildings whose shadows you cannot see.

What it calculates

One quantity, reported two ways. Irradiance in W/m² for a single date and time, and cumulative radiation in kWh/m² accumulated hour by hour over a period you choose — full year, a season, a month, a day, or a custom range, with an hour window on top.

Every analysis reduces to the same problem: a set of directions, each carrying an amount of energy, plus a visibility test per sensor. The sky patches, the reflecting ground and the sun are folded into one flat source list so the ray tracer has a single code path, and a sensor's value is a cosine-weighted sum over that list with blocked directions dropped.

The beam is deliberately kept out of the sky patches. Smearing a low-altitude sun across 145 patches is a known artefact of the coarse gendaymtx default, and the code says it is avoiding exactly that. The direct term keeps its angular sharpness at low sun — which is precisely where overshadowing studies live.

Not modelled at all

The big one
No interreflection of any kind
A ray that meets a surface is blocked, never bounced. Every façade opposite yours takes light away and returns none, so courtyards, light wells, atria and urban canyons read systematically low, in one direction, by an amount the tool cannot estimate.
Out
Material properties, and transparency
No absorptance, reflectance, colour or emissivity: a sensor reports the irradiance arriving, never what the surface does with it. Nothing is transparent either — a window is a solid triangle that blocks the beam completely.
Out
Turbidity, spectrum, snow
Broadband throughout, with no turbidity control anywhere in the interface. Ground reflectance is one number for the whole run, so a winter figure for a snow-covered site is wrong in a direction the tool cannot correct.
Partly
Trees
Single trees can be placed by hand: round, conical or columnar, evergreen or deciduous. They shade but are never measured. In leaf a crown is solid, so its shade is a little overstated; out of leaf (outside 1 May – 31 October unless changed) a deciduous tree shades with its trunk and a few bare limbs. Trees in OpenStreetMap are not loaded.
Partly
Terrain, and context
The ground is a flat plane for the reflected term only — a sloping or sunken site is not represented unless you import it as geometry. Context shades but never receives: you cannot read a number off a neighbour.

How exact the numbers are

−0.19%
Mid-hour sampling
The sun is sampled at the midpoint of each hour rather than integrated across it. Measured over a full year against the climate file's own global horizontal. Systematic and small, but it is the floor on any annual total.
2.5°
Sun-bin resolution
Over a period, sun-up hours are binned onto a 2.5° grid and each bin becomes one energy-weighted direction. Cumulative shadow edges are quantised at roughly ten minutes of solar movement.
binary
No penumbra
Visibility is a hard 0 or 1 per direction, with no sun disc and no partial occlusion — so there is no penumbra, the soft band along a real shadow edge where the sun, being a disc about half a degree wide, is only partly hidden. A sensor near an edge can differ sharply from its neighbour. Read the field, never the point.
120,000
Sensor ceiling
Above it the tool coarsens the grid rather than freeze, and says so in a toast. If that fired, the grid size in your CSV is the one you asked for, not the one that ran.
99.6%
Looked-up time zone
Searching for a place reads its standard UTC offset from a 0.25° raster of the IANA boundaries carried in the page — no network, no key. It agrees with the recorded zone for 99.6 per cent of the 34,006 cities of GeoNames cities15000, against 58.9 per cent for the round(lon / 15) estimate it replaces. The residue is towns within a few kilometres of a boundary, which a 28 km cell cannot separate. A wrong offset shifts every sun position in the file by a whole hour while leaving all of them looking entirely plausible.
26 m
Looked-up elevation
Read from the same grid: ETOPO1 topography in 50 m steps, clamped to 0–2,500 m, the range Hottel's fit covers. Median error against the 24 preset cities is 26 m and the worst 183 m, at most 4.8 per cent of the beam. ETOPO1 is smoothed over roughly 28 km, so summits are flattened and valley floors lifted — it gives the height of a region, not of a site. It matters only under the clear-sky fallback, where elevation enters through Hottel's A term; a climate file carries its own.

These figures are internal consistency, not validation. They come from the tool's own suite driving the shipped file in headless Chromium. There is no Radiance cross-run behind any number, and no comparison against measured site data. That establishes agreement with closed-form geometry, which is a weaker claim than validation and should be described as such.

The two site lookups are flagged, not trusted. They are also the only rows above measured against outside reference data rather than against the tool itself — recorded zones and hand-checked elevations — which is why they carry a hit rate and an error rather than a bias. A searched place shows a note under the time-zone field and another under the elevation field until you edit the value or save the location, because a 0.25° cell can neither separate the two sides of a zone boundary nor tell a valley floor from the ridge above it. Both are starting points to confirm, not readings to cite. The full notes give the derivation of each.

Against Radiance and Ladybug

Provenance. Everything else here is read from the source. This section is not, and cannot be: the code says nothing about how those tools behave. It reasons from what the code does — check it against their documentation before relying on it.

Should agree on the diffuse term. The Perez coefficient table and its parameter equations are transcribed from gendaymtx.c, and the sky patch construction is rh_init()'s, to the same formula.

Will diverge on anything relying on reflection — the single largest source of divergence, and one-directional. Also on the direct term at low sun, where this tool bins the beam separately at 2.5° while a default gendaymtx run smears it across the patches, so a comparison must say which Radiance configuration is on the other side.

Three exercises

Each isolates one variable and holds everything else fixed. Unless told otherwise, work in London at the default coordinates, grid size 1.0 m, sky Tregenza (145), ground reflectance 0.20, and let each run finish before reading the legend.

Exercise 01

A neighbour that costs you nothing at noon can still take your afternoon

  1. Load the Tower with context sample. Date 21 June, 12:00, mode Instant W/m². Run, then open Results and find the By orientation table. Its columns are Face, Area m², Mean and Max: there is no per-face minimum, and the Minimum above it is over the whole model. The S row also pools every south-facing surface in the scene — 1,742 m² here, of which only 784 m² is the tower. Record its Mean.
  2. Change to 21 December, same time, re-run and record again. Noon sun falls from 62.0° to 15.1° — yet on both dates the neighbours cast nothing on the tower's south face. What you recorded is altitude and irradiance, not overshadowing.
  3. Switch to Cumulative, Heating season (Oct–Apr), kWh/m², and set From hour 11, To hour 14. Run; turn Surfaces shade each other off and run again. Over those midday hours the tower's south face barely moves — about half a per cent.
  4. Set From hour 14, To hour 17 and repeat. Now it loses about 23% to the neighbours. Put the hours back to 0 and 24 and compare the heating season against the cooling season.

Principle. Overshadowing is a function of altitude and azimuth, and the azimuth decides whether a neighbour is your problem at all. At noon the sun is due south, so an obstruction that is not due south throws its shadow past you — and none of these four is on the noon line. A noon check is not an overshadowing check; it is the hour most likely to report that everything is fine. What winter adds is length: at 15° a block throws a shadow nearly four times its height, at 5° nearly ten times, so a low sun and an off-axis neighbour meet in the hours either side of noon. Across the whole heating season these four take 15% of the south-facing total in the scene, against 10% over the cooling season.

Exercise 02

Overhang depth is set by the summer altitude, not the equinox

  1. Load the Shaded facade study sample, grid size 0.5 m. Look at it first: the south face carries five horizontal louvres 2.2 m deep at 3 m centres and five vertical fins. Nothing in this tool is glazed — the analysis surface is the face itself.
  2. Set 21 March (day 80), 12:00, run an instant analysis and note what fraction of the face is in shadow: about 65%. Repeat at 21 June (about 85%) and 21 December (about 32%).
  3. Run three cumulative passes on the same geometry — Cooling season, then Heating season, then Full year — recording the façade mean for each.
  4. Turn on Cast shadows, set Animate day and play a June day, then a December day, watching where the shadow line sits at noon.

Principle. A horizontal louvre intercepts sun in proportion to how steeply it arrives, which is why the shaded fraction tracks the noon altitude almost exactly: 15°, 39°, 62° give 32%, 65%, 85%. Sized at the equinox the array is far too shallow in June and needlessly obstructive in December; sized at the summer solstice it works when cooling load is highest and steps aside when the sun drops. The right question is never “how deep should an overhang be” but “at what altitude should it stop shading”. The seasonal totals say it from the other end: this array removes about 80% of the clear-sky beam over the cooling season and about 57% over the heating season. The vertical fins are part of that — they catch the low morning and afternoon sun that passes under every horizontal louvre.

Exercise 03

East and west are identical under a clear sky and unequal under a real climate

  1. Load the Single box (reference) sample with no EPW loaded, so the clear-sky fallback is running — the panel says so.
  2. Run cumulative, Full year, kWh/m². Record the mean on the east façade and on the west. They will be close to equal — about 0.15% apart, and that residue is only the run stepping whole hours of standard time while solar noon drifts through the year. The clear-sky model reads nothing but the sun's altitude, so the synthetic day is symmetric about solar noon.
  3. Import a real EPW for the same city, re-run the identical analysis, and record east and west again. Take the difference as a percentage of the east value.
  4. Repeat for a climate from a different region — somewhere with a monsoon, or with morning coastal fog — and compare the sign and size of the asymmetry.

Principle. Solar geometry is symmetric about noon; weather is not. Morning cloud, afternoon convective build-up, coastal fog and urban haze all break the symmetry, and the direction they break it in is a property of the climate rather than of the latitude. This is the clearest demonstration of why a clear-sky result cannot stand in for a climate result — the two disagree not by a scale factor but by a shape, and no correction applied to the symmetric answer would recover the real one.

A caution for all three: read the field, not the point. Sensors sit at sub-triangle centroids, and a single one near a shadow edge can differ sharply from its neighbours. Quote means over a façade, and quote differences between runs rather than absolute values.

Where it stops

The tool is for teaching relationships and orders of magnitude — how overshadowing moves with the season, why a device that works on one orientation fails on another, what a real climate does to a symmetric geometry. For a compliance submission, an energy model, or any figure a consultant will stake a recommendation on, use a validated tool. The absence of interreflection alone is too large for this one to serve that purpose.

References

The sources the code itself names, and the methods it names by name alone. The code names each source but rarely its bibliographic detail, so that was added from the published record.

Solar position

Meeus, J. (1998). Astronomical Algorithms, 2nd edn. Willmann-Bell. ISBN 978-0-943396-61-3, chapters 25 and 28. — The most specific citation in the file, naming edition and chapters. Every sun position the tool computes.

NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory Solar Calculator. — The low-precision arrangement of Meeus's algorithm, and the three-branch refraction fit.

The sky

Perez, R., Seals, R. & Michalsky, J. (1993). ‘All-weather model for sky luminance distribution’, Solar Energy, 50(3), pp. 235–245. — The eight-bin, twenty-coefficient table is that paper's Table 1, and the code names the table explicitly. Every sky the tool builds uses it.

Tregenza, P. R. (1987). Lighting Research & Technology, 19(1), pp. 13–14; and Reinhart, C. F. & Walkenhorst, O. (2001). Energy and Buildings, 33(7), pp. 683–697. — The 145-patch subdivision and its refinement to 577. Named by name only: the code says “Tregenza (145)” and “Reinhart (577)” with no year and no paper, so these are attributions supplied from the published record rather than citations the code makes.

The clear-sky fallback

Hottel, H. C. (1976). Solar Energy, 18, pp. 129–134; and Liu, B. Y. H. & Jordan, R. C. (1960). Solar Energy, 4(3), pp. 1–19. — Beam transmittance for the 23 km visibility atmosphere, and the diffuse correlation. Together these are the synthetic clear day.

Kasten, F. (1965). Archiv für Meteorologie, Geophysik und Bioklimatologie B, 14, pp. 206–223. — Relative optical air mass. The edition matters: the code implements Kasten's 1965 coefficients, not the widely cited Kasten & Young 1989 revision, which uses different constants.

Implementation

Radiance — Ward, G. J. (1994). ‘The RADIANCE lighting simulation and rendering system’, Proceedings of SIGGRAPH ’94, pp. 459–472. — The code names the source files and functions rather than the paper: the coefficient table is transcribed from gendaymtx.c and the patch construction from rh_init().

Möller, T. & Trumbore, B. (1997). Journal of Graphics Tools, 2(1), pp. 21–28. — The ray-triangle test at every leaf of the bounding-volume hierarchy, reduced to its occlusion form. Named by name only.

Data and services

EnergyPlus Weather (EPW) format, U.S. Department of Energy; Ladybug Tools (ladybug/epw.py); OpenStreetMap and the Overpass API under ODbL; Nominatim. — The climate file layout and the column positions verified against it, the building footprints and heights, and place search. Nominatim takes no part in any calculation.

Named, but not cited

Three works sit outside both lists and it is better to say why. Perez et al. (1990) is the usual source for the clearness and brightness parameterisation the code computes, but the code cites only the 1993 paper. Bourgeois, Reinhart & Ward (2008) is where the MF:n generalisation the code implements was formalised, and the code names only “Reinhart”. And ASHRAE is deliberately absent: until version 1.1.0 the export header described the clear-sky fallback as “ASHRAE/Hottel”, which was wrong — the ASHRAE model uses tabulated optical depths, none of which is present. The label has been corrected and no ASHRAE method is claimed anywhere.
