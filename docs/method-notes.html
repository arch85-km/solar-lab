Solar Analysis Lab - Method Notes

What this tool computes, how it computes it, and — the part that matters most when you cite it — what it does not model at all. Everything below is read from the source code and the tool's own validation run, not from what a solar analysis tool is generally assumed to do.

Audience Architecture students and staff Engines Perez all-weather sky · BVH ray tracing Runs Entirely in the browser Version 1.0.0 · 16 September 2026

Four things to know before you cite a number

  1. What the eye sees and what the number reports are two different machineries. The shadows in the viewport are a GPU shadow map of fixed size; the analysis is a ray test against a bounding-volume hierarchy, run in a worker. They can disagree at the edges, and when they do the number is the one to trust.
  2. With no EPW loaded, every figure is a synthetic clear day. The fallback is a fair-weather upper bound with no cloud in it at all. A kWh/m² total produced that way is not a climate result and must never be quoted as one.
  3. The OpenStreetMap context shades the analysis but is never measured. Sensors come from your model alone, so the neighbours cast onto it and receive nothing. The switch that lets them shade is on by default, which is the opposite of what most people assume — a run with context loaded is already a shaded run.
  4. Only the near ring of context casts a viewport shadow. Buildings within 160 m are in the shadow map; the rest are not, because a frustum stretched over 600 m of city would blur the model's own shadows. The ray tracer sees all of them regardless, so the numbers account for buildings whose shadows you cannot see.

Irradiance at a sensor

Every analysis reduces to the same problem, and the code says so in as many words: a set of directions, each carrying an amount of energy, plus a visibility test per sensor. Direct sun, the diffuse sky patches and the reflecting ground are folded into one flat source list so the ray tracer has a single code path. The value at a sensor is then a cosine-weighted sum over that list, with blocked directions dropped:

E(p) = Σⱼ Vⱼ · max(0, n̂ₚ · d̂ⱼ) · vis(p, d̂ⱼ) W/m² j runs over every source: the sky patches, the ground patches, and the sun Vⱼ the energy carried by direction j, already multiplied by that patch's solid angle n̂ₚ the sensor's outward normal — back-facing directions contribute nothing, they are not merely attenuated vis 0 if any triangle blocks the ray, 1 otherwise; a binary any-hit test, with no partial occlusion

The three groups of sources are built as follows.

diffuse sky Vᵢ = Lᵢ · ωᵢ Lᵢ from the Perez distribution, ωᵢ the patch solid angle. 145 patches, or 577 at Reinhart density. ground Vᵢ = (ρ · GHI / π) · ωᵢ The ground is the sky dome mirrored below the horizon, at a single uniform radiance. ρ is the ground reflectance, default 0.20. direct beam V = DNI One direction for an instant. Over a period, every sun-up hour is binned onto a 2.5° grid and the bins are energy-weighted; the test suite reduces 4,703 sun-up hours to 589 unique directions.

The beam is deliberately kept out of the sky patches. Smearing a low-altitude sun across 145 Tregenza patches is a well-known artefact of the coarse gendaymtx default, and the code says it is avoiding exactly that. The consequence for you is that the direct term keeps its angular sharpness at low sun — which is precisely where overshadowing studies live.

Cumulative runs accumulate the same source list over every hour in the period, giving Wh/m², which is divided by 1,000 for the kWh/m² display or by the hour count for the average-irradiance display. The ray trace itself runs once, against the accumulated source list, not once per hour.

02

The solar position

The code names its source in the module header: the NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory solar calculator, after Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms, 2nd edition, chapters 25 and 28. It is the low-precision form of that algorithm, which is what NOAA's own calculator uses.

The Julian day is computed for the civil date at 00:00 UT with the standard Gregorian correction, then converted to Julian centuries since J2000.0. From that come the mean longitude, mean anomaly, orbital eccentricity, the equation of centre, true and apparent longitude with the nutation term in Ω, the obliquity of the ecliptic and its correction, and finally the declination. The equation of time is the usual series in y = tan²(ε/2):

EoT = 4 · (y·sin 2L₀ − 2e·sin M + 4ey·sin M·cos 2L₀ − ½y²·sin 4L₀ − 1.25e²·sin 2M) minutes

Refraction

NOAA's piecewise fit is applied to the true altitude to give the apparent altitude the tool reports. It has three branches and an upper cut-off, and the altitude ranges are exactly these:

 Result in arcseconds, divided by 3600. Every altitude the tool displays or uses for shading is the refracted one.

Clock time, time zone and daylight saving

The site carries a standard-time offset from UTC, in hours, adjustable from −12 to +14 in half-hour steps. Daylight saving is a separate switch worth one hour. The code subtracts it before doing any astronomy — stdMinutes = minutes − dstHours × 60 — so the switch shifts the clock and never the sun. True solar time then adds the equation of time and the longitude correction of four minutes per degree.

What you see is the switch labelled Daylight saving (+1h), off by default, and a clock that jumps by an hour when you use it while the sun stays where it was. Sunrise, sunset and solar noon are reported in local clock time and move with it.

Sunrise, sunset and day length

Computed at the conventional 90.833° zenith, which carries the standard allowance for refraction and the sun's semi-diameter. Polar day and polar night are detected explicitly and reported as such rather than producing a spurious time.

Checked against published values

03

The sky model

a. Sky discretisation

The sky vault is divided into Tregenza/Reinhart patches. The module header states that it is generated exactly as Radiance's rh_init() does in gendaymtx.c, and the construction is reproduced here in full because it is short enough to check:

α = (π/2) / (7·MF + 0.5) row i altitude = α · (i + 0.5) patches in row = tnaz[⌊i/MF⌋] · MF, tnaz = [30,30,24,24,18,12,6] solid angle = 2π · (sin α(i+1) − sin αi) / patchesInRow zenith cap = 2π · (1 − cos(α/2))

MF:1 gives 145 patches, which is Tregenza's subdivision unchanged; MF:2 gives 577, which is Reinhart's. Both counts include the single zenith cap. The interface offers exactly these two, labelled Tregenza (145) and Reinhart (577). The solid angle is integrated over the band rather than evaluated at the patch centre, and the suite confirms the patch set sums to a hemisphere: 6.283185 sr against 2π.

b. The all-weather sky

The Perez model supplies the relative radiance of each patch. The module header states that the coefficient table and the parameter equations are transcribed verbatim from Radiance's gendaymtx.c — specifically PerezCoeff, CalcPerezParam and CalcRelLuminance — and the full eight-bin, twenty-coefficient table is present in the file.

L(γ, ζ) = (1 + a·exp(b / cos ζ)) · (1 + c·exp(d·γ) + e·cos²γ) γ angle between the sky point and the sun ζ zenith angle of the sky point, with cos ζ floored at 0.01

Clearness ε selects the bin. The upper bounds are 1.065, 1.230, 1.500, 1.950, 2.800, 4.500, 6.200 and above, with ε itself clamped to the range 1.0 to 11.9. Brightness Δ uses the air mass and the eccentricity correction, clamped to 0.01–0.6.

Two details of Radiance's implementation are carried across rather than simplified away. The brightness is clamped for the intermediate clearness bins — if (ε > 1.065 && ε < 2.8 && Δ < 0.2) Δ = 0.2 — and in the lowest bin, the overcast one, the coefficients c and d are computed from Perez's equations 7 and 8 instead of the linear equation 6 used for every other bin:

c = exp( (Δ(x₃₀ + x₃₁·Z))^x₃₂ ) − x₃₃ d = −exp( Δ(x₄₀ + x₄₁·Z) ) + x₄₂ + Δ·x₄₃

The air mass is what the code calls a Kasten-style relative optical air mass, identified with Radiance's CalcAirMass() rather than with any publication:

m = 1 / (cos Z + 0.15 · max(93.885 − Z°, 0.1)^−1.253)

Those coefficients are Kasten's 1965 set, not the 1989 Kasten & Young revision that is more often cited. The distinction matters if you compare this term against another implementation, and the references below set it out. It is used only to compute the Perez brightness parameter Δ, so its influence on a result is indirect.

Normalisation

Relative radiances mean nothing on their own, so the patch set is scaled to reproduce the measured diffuse horizontal irradiance exactly. The sum of radiance × solid angle × cos ζ over all patches is computed, and every patch is divided by it and multiplied by DHI. If that sum degenerates the code falls back to a uniform sky rather than dividing by something near zero. The suite confirms the round trip at four solar altitudes — 12°, 30°, 55° and 78° — recovering the input DHI to two decimal places in every case.

c. The clear-sky fallback

When no EPW is loaded, and only then, the hourly irradiance comes from a synthetic clear day. Beam transmittance is Hottel's 1976 correlation for the 23 km visibility standard atmosphere, with the mid-latitude summer correction factors applied to each constant:

a₀ = 0.97 · (0.4237 − 0.00821(6 − A)²) a₁ = 0.99 · (0.5055 + 0.00595(6.5 − A)²) k = 1.02 · (0.2711 + 0.01858(2.5 − A)²) τ_b = a₀ + a₁ · exp(−k / cos Z) DNI = G_on · τ_b, G_on = 1367 · (1 + 0.033 cos(2π·doy/365))

A is site elevation in kilometres, clamped to the range 0–2.5 km because that is where Hottel's fit is valid — the code says so at the clamp. The diffuse fraction is the Liu & Jordan 1960 correlation, τ_d = 0.271 − 0.294·τ_b, and global horizontal follows as DNI·cos Z + DHI. Solar altitude is floored at a cosine of 0.02 so the model does not diverge at the horizon.

What to conclude from a cumulative figure produced this way. That it is an upper bound on a fair-weather site, and nothing more. There is no cloud in this model, no aerosol variation, no seasonal turbidity and no year-to-year variability. A London roof will read far above anything the real climate delivers. Use it to compare two designs on the same synthetic day, which is legitimate and useful; do not use it to predict a yield, and do not describe it as a climate result. The tool says as much on screen while it is in use.

Where the irradiance comes from

An EPW file, when you load one. The parser reads a deliberately small subset of the format, and the module header records which reference the field positions were checked against: ladybug/epw.py, 0-based.

IndexFieldUsed for
1MonthMapping the row to a day of year
2DayAs above
3Hour, 1–24The hour slot within that day
6Dry bulb temperature, °CThe climate summary only — it enters no calculation
13Global horizontal irradiance, Wh/m²The ground-reflected term, and the live cross-check
14Direct normal irradiance, Wh/m²The beam
15Diffuse horizontal irradiance, Wh/m²Normalising the Perez sky
 The LOCATION header supplies city, state, country, source, latitude, longitude, time zone and elevation, and loading a file moves the site to match. Everything else in the format is ignored.

Values at or above 9990 are treated as the radiation missing-value sentinel and zeroed; dry bulb is rejected at an absolute value of 99 or more. 29 February is dropped, so the year is always 8,760 hours. A file yielding fewer than 8,000 valid hourly records is rejected outright with a message saying how many it found, rather than being silently analysed with holes in it.

The hour convention

This is the detail most likely to put a result out by an hour, and the code states it at both places it matters: EPW hour h describes the hour preceding h. So hour 1 covers midnight to 01:00 and lands in index 0. For an instant, the tool takes hourIndex = ⌊minutes / 60⌋. For a cumulative run it samples the middle of each hour:

minutes = h · 60 + 30 mid-hour: EPW hour h covers (h−1, h]

The sun position for that hour is therefore computed at its midpoint, not its start or end, which is the right choice for a quantity that is an hourly mean — but it does mean the sun moves in half-hour steps within the accumulation, and a shadow edge sweeping across a façade is resolved no finer than that.

What the parser reports back

Loading a file produces a summary: annual global horizontal, direct normal and diffuse horizontal totals in kWh/m²; the number of hours with any radiation at all, which the tool calls sun-up hours; and the minimum, maximum and mean dry-bulb temperature. For the Chicago O'Hare file the suite uses, sun-up hours come to 4,703.

The live cross-check

The tool continuously computes the irradiance an unobstructed upward-facing surface would receive from the current source list, and compares it with the global horizontal the climate file reports for the same hour. The two must agree when an EPW is loaded, because the sky matrix is built to reproduce DHI exactly and the beam is added at its own measured DNI — if they diverged, the sky assembly would be wrong. Measured over a full year, the suite finds the sampled sum at 203,410 W/m² against the file's 203,788, a difference of −0.19%, which is the mid-hour sampling and nothing else.

A second measured check of the same machinery: a downward-facing surface reads 180.0 W/m² where reflectance × GHI predicts 179.0, and roof annual radiation comes to 1,403 kWh/m² against a site global horizontal of 1,406.6. Both confirm the ground term and the accumulated sky are scaled correctly.

Not modelled

These are the assumptions a student is most likely to make wrongly, because a general-purpose solar tool would handle them. Each one is traceable to something the code does not do, not to a general expectation of what such tools omit.

Out
Reflected sun from adjacent façades
There is no interreflection of any kind. The source list contains the sky, the ground and the sun, and a ray that hits a triangle is simply blocked — it does not bounce. A glazed tower opposite will take light away from your façade and give none back, and the CSV header says No interreflection in as many words. Every surface is a diffuse receiver only.
Out
Material properties of any kind
No absorptance, no reflectance on the model itself, no colour, no emissivity. A sensor reports the irradiance arriving at it, never what the surface does with it. The ground reflectance is the only material number in the tool, and it belongs to the ground plane rather than to any surface you draw.
Out
Glazing and transmittance
Nothing is transparent. A window is a solid triangle that blocks the beam completely, so nothing about internal daylight or solar gain through glass can be read from this tool. Its sister tool exists for that.
Out
Spectral effects
Broadband throughout. No wavelength dependence, no separate visible and near-infrared bands, no colour of sky or sun.
Out
Atmospheric turbidity as a variable
Under a climate file, turbidity enters only as whatever is already implicit in the measured DNI and DHI. Under the clear-sky fallback it is fixed by Hottel's 23 km visibility atmosphere and cannot be adjusted — there is no turbidity input anywhere in the interface.
Out
Altitude correction beyond Hottel's
Site elevation enters the clear-sky model only, through Hottel's A term, clamped to 2.5 km. With an EPW loaded, elevation is recorded from the header and used for nothing at all.
Out
Snow albedo, seasonal or otherwise
Ground reflectance is a single number for the whole run. It cannot vary by month, so a winter cumulative figure for a snow-covered site is wrong in a direction the tool cannot correct.
Out
Tree canopy and vegetation
No trees, no partial transmission, no seasonal leaf cover. Overpass is queried for buildings alone. A deciduous street would have to be modelled as OBJ solids, and would then shade the whole year identically.
Out
Sensors on context geometry
Sensors come from your model alone. You cannot read a number off a neighbour, and the context contributes no measured area to any statistic.
Note
Calendar
A flat 365-day year. 29 February is dropped from any EPW, and daylight saving shifts the clock for the instantaneous view but is applied throughout a cumulative run as a single constant offset, not as a seasonal rule.
06

Resolution and accuracy

a. The sensor grid

There is no rectangular workplane here. Sensors are generated from the model's own triangles by recursive four-way midpoint subdivision: each triangle is split until its area falls below gridSize² / 2, which is the area of a right triangle with an edge of the target size. Subdivision is capped at seven levels. Each resulting sub-triangle contributes one sensor at its centroid, pushed off the surface along the normal by the offset distance, and carries its own area so that area-weighted statistics are correct on an irregular mesh.

ParameterDefaultRangeNote
Grid size1.5 m0.2 – 20 mTarget edge length, not a spacing
Offset0.02 m0.001 – 1 mLifts the sensor clear of its own surface
 The ceiling is 120,000 sensors. If the requested grid would exceed it, the tool backs every triangle off by the same number of subdivision levels — up to seven — rather than freezing the tab, and says so in a toast. A result that has been coarsened this way is flagged, and the grid size you asked for is not the one you got.

b. The ray test

Occlusion runs in a Web Worker, off the main thread, so the viewport stays interactive and progress can be reported honestly rather than estimated. The worker needs no three.js: it receives raw triangle data, nine floats per triangle, and answers occlusion queries against a bounding-volume hierarchy it builds itself — median split on the centroid along the longest axis of each node, with leaves of four triangles or fewer.

Each leaf test is Möller–Trumbore, and the code notes that it is occlusion only: any hit within the ray's maximum distance wins and the traversal stops. There is no nearest-hit search, because nothing beyond the first blocker matters. Progress is posted roughly sixty times over the sensor loop.

The ray length is capped at six times the model radius, or 500 m, whichever is larger. Geometry beyond that distance cannot block anything — which is generous for a building but worth knowing if you import a site model spanning kilometres.

c. The shadow map — for the eye only

The shadows in the viewport are a three.js shadow map at a fixed 2048 × 2048 texels with PCF soft filtering, cast onto a plane that carries a ShadowMaterial so the basemap shows through untouched everywhere else. The catcher is sized to the model's neighbourhood — 3.2 times the model radius, or 1.15 times the context's shadow ring, whichever is larger — because a frustum sized to the model alone cuts the context's shadows off with a straight edge.

Only context buildings within SHADOW_RADIUS = 160 m cast into that map. The reasoning is in the code: the map is a fixed budget of texels, so a frustum stretched over 600 m of city would blur the model's own shadows, and a block 200 m away only reaches the site when the sun is under about 10°.

None of this feeds the numbers. The shadow map is a rendering of the scene; the analysis is the ray test described above, which sees all 1,200 context buildings whatever the map shows. The catcher is explicitly marked as excluded from analysis so it never becomes a sensor surface itself. If a façade reads as shaded in the number but lit in the viewport, the far ring is the reason.

d. What the browser approximations cost

−0.19%
Mid-hour sampling
Sampling the sun at the midpoint of each hour rather than integrating across it. Measured over a full year against the climate file's own global horizontal: 203,410 W/m² sampled against 203,788 reported. Systematic and small, but it is the floor on any annual total.
2.5°
Sun-bin resolution
Over a period, sun-up hours are binned onto a 2.5° grid in altitude and azimuth and each bin becomes one energy-weighted direction — 589 directions from 4,703 hours in the measured case. Shadow edges in a cumulative map are therefore quantised at roughly 2.5° of solar movement, about ten minutes of clock time.
145 vs 577
Sky density
Tregenza is the default. Reinhart quarters each patch, at four times the tracing cost, and matters most where a small patch of sky is visible through a narrow gap. For an open site the difference is slight; for a courtyard or a light well it is worth checking both.
binary
No penumbra
Visibility is a hard 0 or 1 per direction, with no sun disc and no partial occlusion. Softness in a cumulative map comes only from sources at different angles being blocked differently, never from a single source being half-blocked.
190 ms
Dragging the time slider
Moving the scrubber rebuilds the sky and redraws many times a second. The analysis does not follow it: re-running is manual by default. Auto makes it follow, and when it does the tool forces instantaneous mode and debounces at 190 ms, so a drag triggers one run when you stop rather than one per frame. The sky dome itself is cached and only rebuilt when the density changes.

What is measured, and what is not

Limitations of the validation

The figures quoted in sections 02, 04 and 06 come from the tool's own suite, which drives the shipped file in headless Chromium. That establishes internal consistency and agreement with closed-form geometry. It is not validation against an independent reference. There is no Radiance cross-run behind any number on this page, and no comparison against measured site data. Section 09, which sets this tool beside Radiance and Ladybug, is reasoning from what the code does rather than a measurement, and says so where it begins.

What the tool is for. Teaching relationships and orders of magnitude — how overshadowing moves with the season, why a device that works on one orientation fails on another, what a real climate does to a symmetric geometry. For a compliance submission, an energy model, or any figure a consultant will stake a recommendation on, use a validated tool. The absence of interreflection alone is too large for this one to serve that purpose.

Diagnostics

Two things the app will tell you about itself, which matter when it runs slowly on a particular machine. At launch it names the graphics renderer in the browser console, and warns if that name is a software rasteriser — a browser with hardware acceleration switched off draws every pixel on the processor and will stutter on any computer, however fast. Adding ?perf=1 to the URL puts a live frame rate beside that name in the corner of the viewport; it reads idle until you move something, because the viewport draws only when the scene changes.

If a student reports that the model will not turn smoothly, the renderer name is the first thing to ask for. It is usually the browser's graphics settings rather than the model, and no change to the geometry will help.

09

Compared with Ladybug and Radiance

Provenance of this section. Everything else on this page is read from the source code. This section is not, and cannot be: the code says nothing about how Radiance or Ladybug behave in practice. What follows reasons from what the code does to what should follow, so check it against those tools' own documentation before relying on it. The figures quoted elsewhere on this page are measured; none in this section are.

The file header states the intent plainly — the methodology follows the Radiance and Ladybug Tools chain — and the correspondence is close enough to be worth setting out term by term.

Where results should agree

  • The diffuse term. The Perez coefficient table and its parameter equations are transcribed from gendaymtx.c, including the brightness clamp for the intermediate bins and the alternative expressions for c and d in the overcast bin. The sky patch construction is rh_init()'s, to the same formula. For the same DNI and DHI, the patch radiances should match Radiance's closely, and any residual is arithmetic rather than method.
  • Solar geometry. NOAA after Meeus, agreeing with published sunrise and sunset to about a minute. No meaningful divergence from any tool.
  • Simple unobstructed cases. A horizontal surface reproduces the climate file's own global horizontal to −0.19% over a year, and an annual roof total lands within 0.3% of the site global horizontal. A flat unshaded surface should agree with a Ladybug incident-radiation study closely.

Where results will diverge

  • Anything relying on reflection. Radiance is a full global-illumination engine; this tool has no interreflection whatever. An urban canyon with bright façades, a courtyard, a light well or any surface facing a reflective neighbour will read low here and there is no setting that closes the gap. This is the single largest source of divergence and it is one-directional.
  • The direct term at low sun. Radiance's gendaymtx by default smears the beam across the sky patches; this tool bins it separately at 2.5°. Its direct component should therefore be sharper than a default gendaymtx run and closer to a -d direct-only calculation. Comparisons must be explicit about which Radiance configuration is on the other side.
  • Shadow edges over a period. Mid-hour sampling and 2.5° sun bins quantise a cumulative shadow boundary. A Radiance run at finer time resolution will place that edge more precisely.
  • Materials. Ladybug and Radiance carry reflectance, specularity and transmittance. This tool carries none of them on the model. Any study whose answer depends on what a surface is made of cannot be reproduced here.
  • The sky at twilight, where the two paths here differ from each other. The instantaneous calculation builds a Perez sky only when the sun is above −6°, so civil twilight contributes nothing to an instant reading. The cumulative path applies no such gate: it builds a sky for any hour with positive diffuse horizontal, including hours with the sun below the horizon. For a climate file this is the right behaviour — that diffuse energy is real and measured — but it means an instant reading at dusk and a one-hour cumulative run over the same hour are not computed the same way, and the cumulative one will be the larger.

The Ladybug connection in the code

Ladybug Tools is named twice. The EPW field indices were verified against ladybug/epw.py, which is the reason those column numbers can be trusted. And the default colour scheme, Spectral, is the blue-to-red radiation ramp chosen specifically because it is the one students will recognise from Ladybug in Grasshopper — the code says so at the definition. No Ladybug Tools code is included in the app.

For a compliance submission, an energy model or anything a consultant will stake a recommendation on, use Radiance through Ladybug and Honeybee, IESVE, or another validated tool. This one is built to teach relationships and orders of magnitude, and its omissions — interreflection above all — are too large for it to be anything else.

10

Three exercises

Each isolates one variable and holds everything else fixed. Unless told otherwise, work in London at the default coordinates, grid size 1.0 m, sky Tregenza (145), ground reflectance 0.20, and let each run finish before reading the legend.

Exercise 01

A neighbour that costs you nothing in June can take your winter entirely

  1. Load the Tower with context sample. Set the date to 21 June, time 12:00.
  2. Run an instant analysis. Record the mean and minimum W/m² on the lower south façade.
  3. Change the date to 21 December, keep the time at 12:00, and re-run. Record both figures again.
  4. Now switch to cumulative, Cooling season (May – Sep), unit kWh/m². Run, and record the façade mean.
  5. Switch the period to Heating season (Oct – Apr) and run again.
  6. Finally, turn Surfaces shade each other off and re-run the heating season. That is the same façade with the neighbour removed.

Principle. Overshadowing is a function of solar altitude, and altitude collapses in winter — at London's latitude noon sun falls from about 62° in June to about 15° in December. The same obstruction subtends a far larger part of the winter sky, so a tower that is invisible to a summer study can remove most of the heating-season gain. Step 6 separates the neighbour's contribution from the façade's own self-shading, which is the number you actually want when arguing about a site boundary.

Exercise 02

Overhang depth is set by the summer altitude, not the equinox

  1. Load the Shaded facade study sample. Grid size 0.5 m.
  2. Set the date to 21 March (day 80), time 12:00, and run an instant analysis. Note how much of the glazed area the overhang shades.
  3. Change to 21 June, same time, and re-run. Note the shaded fraction again.
  4. Change to 21 December and re-run. Note it a third time.
  5. Now run three cumulative passes on the same geometry — Cooling season, then Heating season, then Full year — and record the façade mean for each.
  6. Turn on Cast shadows and animate a June day, then a December day, watching where the shadow line sits at noon.

Principle. A horizontal overhang intercepts sun in proportion to how steeply it arrives. Sized at the equinox it will be far too shallow in June and needlessly obstructive in December; sized at the summer solstice it does the work when cooling load is highest and steps out of the way when the sun drops. The right question is never "how deep should an overhang be" but "at what altitude should it stop shading", and steps 5 and 6 show the two seasons pulling in opposite directions.

Exercise 03

East and west are identical under a clear sky and unequal under a real climate

  1. Load the Single box (reference) sample with no EPW loaded, so the clear-sky fallback is running. The panel says so.
  2. Run cumulative, Full year, kWh/m². Record the mean on the east façade and on the west façade.
  3. They will be close to equal. Satisfy yourself that this is geometry: the synthetic day is symmetric about solar noon, so morning and afternoon deliver the same energy.
  4. Now import a real EPW for the same city and re-run the identical analysis.
  5. Record east and west again, and take the difference as a percentage of the east value.
  6. Repeat for a second climate from a different region — somewhere with a monsoon, or with morning coastal fog — and compare the sign and size of the asymmetry.

Principle. Solar geometry is symmetric about noon; weather is not. Morning cloud, afternoon convective build-up, coastal fog and urban haze all break the symmetry, and the direction they break it in is a property of the climate rather than of the latitude. This is the clearest demonstration of why a clear-sky result cannot stand in for a climate result — the two disagree not by a scale factor but by a shape, and no correction applied to the symmetric answer would recover the real one.

A caution for all three: read the field, not the point. Sensors sit at sub-triangle centroids and a single one near a shadow edge can differ sharply from its neighbours, because visibility is binary and there is no penumbra. Quote means over a façade, and quote differences between runs rather than absolute values.
